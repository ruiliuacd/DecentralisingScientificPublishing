# DecentralisingScientificPublishing
can the blockchain improve science communication

今年诺奖生理学奖揭晓，颁给了光遗传学的 Karl Deisseroth 等人。而华人科学家潘卓华，可能是光遗传学的最早开创者，2004 年 7 月，潘卓华使用病毒感染了第一只老鼠，大约五周之后观察其视网膜，呈现在他眼前的是一片绿色的海洋——视网膜上成千上万的神经节细胞都拥有带绿色荧光的通道视紫红质（ChR）。他向这些细胞中植入监测电极，然后进行光照，细胞反馈了一阵电活动—— ChR 起作用了。

2004 年 11 月 25 日，潘卓华把这项工作投稿给了 Nature 期刊，但 Nature 期刊编辑建议他转投到 Nature 旗下的神经科学期刊  Nature Neuroscience 上，但转投后却遭到拒稿。2005 年初，潘卓华又将论文投到 Journal of Neuroscience 期刊，但依然被拒稿。

2005 年 5 月，潘卓华在视觉和眼科学研究协会（Association for Research in Vision and Opthamology）的会议上介绍了这项在神经元中使用 ChR 的工作。

仅仅几个月后（2005 年 8 月），Nature Neuroscience 期刊发表了 Karl Deisseroth 团队的使用 ChR 让神经元对光敏感的论文。

而在 2006 年 4 月，潘卓华的论文终于在 Neuron 期刊上发表，仅仅几个月之差，一切都不一样了。Karl Deisseroth 因光遗传学而一飞冲天，接连获得了科学突破奖、哈维奖、盖尔德纳奖、京都奖、拉斯克奖、霍维茨奖、引文桂冠奖...以及今天的诺贝尔奖。而潘卓华，似乎终将被遗忘。

这种例子还有很多，例如crisper的发现者，以及本人的文章都遭遇了重重阻碍，并被他人模仿而发表。当学术成果涉及到巨大利益，学术交流被出版商和‘权势大佬’所操纵，公平和真正的学术创新如何存在值得深思。

除了抄袭剽窃洗稿与学术造假以外，真实的学术成果是否可以通过现在的公开出版进行传播，尤其是考虑到现在提出的所谓科技‘脱钩’。我们是否需要一个去中心化的体系来建立学术交流新机制值得思考。

This year, the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was announced, and it was awarded to Karl Deisseroth and others for their work on optogenetics. The Chinese scientist Zhuo-Hua Pan, however, may have been one of the earliest pioneers of optogenetics. In July 2004, Pan used a virus to infect the first mouse. About five weeks later, when he examined its retina, what appeared before his eyes was a sea of green: thousands upon thousands of retinal ganglion cells were expressing channelrhodopsin (ChR), a light-sensitive channel protein that fluoresced green. He implanted recording electrodes into these cells and then exposed them to light. The cells responded with a burst of electrical activity—ChR was working.

On November 25, 2004, Pan submitted this work to *Nature*. The editors of *Nature* suggested that he transfer the manuscript to *Nature Neuroscience*, a neuroscience journal published by the same group. After the transfer, however, the manuscript was rejected. In early 2005, Pan submitted the paper to the *Journal of Neuroscience*, but it was rejected again.

In May 2005, Pan presented this work on the use of ChR in neurons at the meeting of the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology (ARVO).

Only a few months later, in August 2005, *Nature Neuroscience* published a paper from Karl Deisseroth's group demonstrating the use of ChR to make neurons sensitive to light.

Then, in April 2006, Pan's paper was finally published in *Neuron*. A difference of only a few months changed everything. Karl Deisseroth rose to prominence through optogenetics and went on to receive the Breakthrough Prize, the Harvey Prize, the Gairdner Award, the Kyoto Prize, the Lasker Award, the Horwitz Prize, the Citation Laureate distinction, and, ultimately, the Nobel Prize awarded today. Pan, by contrast, seems destined to be forgotten.

There are many other examples. The discoverers of CRISPR, for instance, also faced considerable obstacles, and some researchers have seen their own work encounter repeated barriers, only to find it imitated and published by others. When scientific discoveries involve enormous potential benefits and interests, and when academic communication is controlled by publishers and powerful figures, it is worth asking how fairness and genuine scientific innovation can survive.

Beyond plagiarism, intellectual theft, the repackaging of others' work, and outright scientific misconduct, can genuine scientific discoveries really be disseminated through the current system of academic publishing? This question becomes particularly important in light of the so-called technological “decoupling” now being discussed around the world.

Perhaps we need to think seriously about whether a decentralized system could provide a new mechanism for academic communication—one that allows scientific knowledge to circulate more freely, reduces dependence on centralized gatekeepers, and gives researchers greater control over the dissemination and development of their own work.
